The challenge

A team came to me with a mockup and a request to build an app. My job was to stop the team from solving the wrong problem — then align everyone around what users actually needed.

Why this project matters

This project was valuable not because I designed another enterprise interface, but because I changed the direction of the work. Instead of polishing a pre-decided solution, I reframed the problem, introduced a better process, and helped the team land on a product direction that made sense.

01 — Context

A mockup is not a brief

Another department approached the team because their clients were struggling with product documentation. But before the problem had been defined, a screen mockup was already on the table and the implied ask was simple: build this app.

I pushed back on that assumption. An app was only one possible response, and probably the wrong one if we did not understand what users were actually trying to do. The real design work started by slowing the team down and asking why this pain existed in the first place.

This was the key tension in the project: shifting the team from solution-first thinking to problem-first thinking inside a large corporate environment that was more used to shipping than questioning.

The proposed solution arrived before the problem was understood.
What I inherited
A pre-decided app concept and a team ready to execute.
What I changed
The team's focus from “what should we build?” to “what problem are we solving?”
Why it mattered
Without that reset, we would likely have shipped a neat interface on top of the wrong structure.
02 — Reframing the work

Introducing Design Thinking
as a working method

I organised a short IBM Design Thinking workshop on a Friday afternoon and adapted the format so non-designers could engage with it quickly. The goal was not to perform process theatre. It was to create enough shared understanding for the team to question the existing direction together.

Using persona framing, empathy mapping, and an AS-IS journey map, I helped the group externalise what users were doing, where they were getting stuck, and which assumptions we had been making without evidence. That changed the conversation immediately.

The strongest signal came afterwards: a non-designer colleague took the AS-IS map into client conversations. At that point, the artefacts were no longer “design deliverables” — they had become a shared decision-making tool.

My contribution

03 — Insight

The issue was not “we need an app”
It was “the service is not mobile-first”

After discussing the current experience with clients, we uncovered the actual root issue: the existing documentation service was fundamentally not designed for mobile use. Users were not simply asking for another channel. They were trying to complete tasks in contexts the current information architecture did not support well.

That insight gave the team a much sharper goal. Rather than building a standalone app because it sounded modern, we needed to restructure the product around a mobile-first information architecture.

This is where the project became strategically interesting: the real design move was not screen polish, but changing the framing from channel to architecture.

Journey mapping exposed where the current service broke down on mobile.
Gap analysis used to align the team on what had to change.
04 — Design response

Redesigning the structure
before refining the interface

Once the problem was clear, I led the design work across structure, interface, and implementation detail. I redesigned the information architecture so the content was organised around user tasks rather than internal logic. Then I translated that structure into mobile-friendly flows and interfaces aligned with IBM Design Language.

I also produced detailed annotations for handoff, making the behaviour and edge cases explicit for developers. By that point, the role I was playing was no longer “make the screens look right.” It was closer to setting direction and reducing ambiguity for the whole team.

05 — Advocacy

I did not just design the product
I helped sell the shift

I also led the internal campaign work for the project. That included shaping the narrative, producing the video, and directing the material used to promote the app internally when it was still called Message Finder.

This mattered because the project was not only about shipping an app. It was also about demonstrating that a more user-centred approach could produce something the wider organisation would actually rally behind.
