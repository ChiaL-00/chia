The challenge

Designing a tool for designers themselves — capturing field insights while they are still fresh, before they disappear into notebooks, camera rolls, and walls.

As an intern at IDEO, I was given an unusually open brief: help IDEOers with their design process. Instead of jumping to a solution, I started by watching how field research actually happened — especially what happened immediately after interviews.

What emerged was not a generic productivity problem, but a very specific workflow gap: the moment when insight is most alive had no tool built for it.

01 — Discover

Three frictions in the research workflow

01 — Capture

The best moment to record an insight was also the easiest moment to lose it

Right after an interview — often in the car on the way back — designers had fresh reactions, quotes, and observations. But there was no lightweight tool designed for that moment.

02 — Format

Research lived across disconnected tools

Notes went into notebooks, quotes into recorders, photos into camera rolls, and synthesis into Google Docs or post-it walls. The same project could be spread across multiple personal systems.

03 — Sharing

Collaboration depended on physical space

To organise and share research, teams often needed a wall, time, and everyone in the same room. That made the process powerful — but also fragile and hard to distribute.

02 — Insight

The wall was not the method — it was the symptom

What designers really needed was not a digital wall. They needed a way to capture insights, quotes, and photos in the moment they happened, then structure and share them without waiting to get back to the studio.

That led to a clear product direction: design a mobile tool that mirrors the natural behaviour of field research rather than forcing a new process onto it.

Key product decision: instead of designing around abstract “research management,” I structured the app around the three things designers were already using in the field — a note, a quote, and a photo.

This kept the product legible, lightweight, and close to existing habits.
03 — Design

From field habits to product structure

📝

Note

Capture an insight as a mobile post-it — fast enough for the moment after an interview, without waiting for later synthesis.

💬

Quote

Separate verbatim participant quotes from designer interpretation, so raw evidence and synthesis do not blur together.

📷

Photo

Keep visual context attached to the research moment instead of leaving it buried in the camera roll.

Research, wrapped for sharing

I designed the output as a formatted PDF that could be emailed to teammates immediately. This turned scattered field inputs into a shareable artefact — readable on screen, printable if needed, and usable beyond the wall.

Crafted to feel like IDEO

Beyond function, I added small moments of playfulness and warmth so the product felt culturally aligned with the environment it was built for. The tool needed to be useful, but also recognisably IDEO.

04 — Build

End-to-end ownership in eight weeks

I carried the project through observation, flow design, interface iteration, Xcode implementation, on-device testing, and the final live demo. That end-to-end ownership is part of why this project still matters to me now.

01

Observe

Mapped how designers captured and organised field research in real settings, instead of relying on idealised descriptions.

02

Structure

Turned those observations into a simple information model: note, quote, photo, and share.

03

Build

Implemented the product in Xcode, iterating the interface through prototype refinement and device testing.

04

Demo

Presented the working tool live to IDEOers, gathered feedback, and helped move it into real internal use.

OUTCOME

Shipped, adopted, and shared beyond the studio

Field Report became an in-house tool at IDEO and was later presented at IxDC Shanghai 2012. For an eight-week internship project, the strongest signal was not just that it shipped — but that it was adopted by the people it was designed for.

It remains a meaningful project in my portfolio because it shows something that still defines how I work now: observing carefully, finding the real workflow gap, and carrying an idea all the way into a working product.
