The challenge

How might we define the right problem before building the solution —
and design a tool that fits how designers actually work?

01 — Context

A brief
not a brief

The brief

”How might we facilitate the design process for IDEOers?”

— open, ambitious, and entirely undefined.

With no predefined problem or direction, I started with observation — spending time with designers, understanding how they captured, organised, and shared their work in practice.

02 — Observation

Three things
no one had named.

01

The wall is the bottleneck

Every project space was covered in boards and sticky notes — the primary way IDEO teams synthesise field research. But the wall demands physical space, printed materials, and everyone present at the same time. It's powerful when it works, but it doesn't scale and it can't travel.

02

Tools are fragmented

Every designer had their own system — one lived in Google Docs, another only trusted hand-written notes, a third kept everything as photos. The same team, the same project, no shared structure. Getting to a common view of the research meant translating across formats no one else had set up.

03

Sharing is the actual goal

Findings only have value when the team can act on them together. The post-it wall was the best synthesis method anyone had — and it required a large physical space and everyone present at the same time. The wall wasn't a design choice. It was the only option available.

The wall was not the method. The wall was the only option.

03 — Insight
03

Why an app?
Because it fit the moment.

The three observations pointed to a problem that was mobile, time-sensitive, and fragmented across tools. The solution needed to live in the same context as the problem — in the designer's hand, right after the interview, shareable immediately.

An app wasn't a default choice because I could code. It was a natural fit for what the problem demanded: capture in the moment, structure across formats, and sharing without a wall. The fact that I could build it myself — not hand off a prototype — meant the solution could be tested and iterated as a real product, not a concept.

04 — Validation

The turning
point.

Midway through the internship, I joined a studio team on an actual field research session with a client. What I'd observed from the studio, I now watched happen in real time.


On the drive back, the team was trying to capture notes on their phones and on scraps of paper. In the hotel meeting room that evening, they were piecing it back together from memory. The three frictions I'd identified weren't theoretical — they were happening in a single afternoon, to a team doing exactly the kind of work IDEO is known for.

Turning point

This session confirmed that the problem was real and the direction was right. But it also changed how I thought about the solution. The app needed to work in the car, on the way back from an interview, with one hand — not just at a desk.

The use context sharpened every design decision that followed.

The field trip didn't validate a prototype. It validated a way of thinking.
05 — Execution

Design and build.
Not a prototype.

I designed and built the app myself in Xcode — front-end interface and back-end data model. A prototype would not have been enough; people were going to actually use it, which meant the data structure had to hold up under real conditions.


The back-end was the harder problem. Modelling the relationship between research sessions, notes, quotes, and photos — in a way that stayed coherent when you added, edited, or shared — took as much design thinking as the screens did.


I didn't treat testing as a phase at the end. I brought IDEOers in while the app was still being built and let their feedback shape the interface in real time.

Design

User flow → screens → iteration

Designed every screen from the user flow up. Iterated through multiple rounds with working IDEO designers as testers.

Development

Xcode, front-end and back-end

Built the data model, session logic, photo pipeline, PDF generation, and email sharing — no handoff, no prototype.

Testing

IDEOers in the loop throughout

Testing was continuous, not a sign-off. Feedback shaped the interface while it was being built, not after.

06 — Product

Three gestures.
One field report.

The interface mirrors the three tools designers already carry into field — notebook, recorder, camera — and gives each a native mobile counterpart.

Note

Capture an insight

A digital post-it, created in the moment — timestamped and tied to a session, not scattered across apps.

Photo

Context stays attached

Photos linked directly to research moments — not floating in a camera roll, disconnected from their meaning.

Share

One tap to the team

Results packaged as a formatted PDF and sent by email. No wall needed. No room required.

The output

Field research, formatted for the debrief

Each session generates a shareable PDF — notes, quotes, and photos organised clearly. Designed to replace the wall in the presentation, not to supplement it.

Craft detail

IDEO's culture treats joy in work as a design value. I added deliberate moments of playfulness — small interactions that made the app feel like it belonged in the studio, not just on it.

Outcome

Ambiguity
turned into product.

Field Report was adopted as an in-house tool at IDEO and used by design teams on live client work. It was later presented at IxDC Shanghai 2012 as a practical example of design tools that embed a firm's philosophy into daily practice.

But the more important outcome was what this project proved: that I could take a deliberately open brief, resist the urge to jump to a solution, identify what the actual problem was — and then build something real. Not a prototype. A shipped product that people chose to use.
