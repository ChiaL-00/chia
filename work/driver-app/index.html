The challenge

How might we improve the Driver App so drivers are more successful at delivering good service as well as achieving their goals — creating a win-win for customers, drivers, and the business?

Overview

A complete redesign of the tool drivers depend on every day

A London-based premium private hire company operates a large fleet of professional drivers across the city. The existing Driver App was the central tool through which drivers received jobs, tracked earnings, and communicated with support — yet it was failing at all three.

I was brought in to diagnose the problems and redesign the product end-to-end — navigating a complex stakeholder ecosystem of call center agents, operations staff, and passengers, while keeping the driver at the centre. They are the only people customers ever actually meet, and without them, there is no business.

01 — Discover

Getting in the car to understand the job

I got in cabs with drivers, sat with call center agents, attended driver training, and read through App Store reviews to understand where the experience was breaking down.

Shadowing the operations and support teams was equally important — drivers don't exist in isolation, and understanding the full ecosystem was the only way to design something that worked for everyone in it.

Pain Points

Four problems undermining every shift

01
Information Architecture

The landing page was a news feed that nobody read. The most important section — the Job List — required two taps to reach. Help and Job History were buried in a hamburger menu that mixed critical and trivial functions together.

02
Confusing Visual Language

Three different UI patterns — a legacy Android design, a custom dark theme, and modal dialogs — coexisted without coherence. Colours carried no consistent meaning. Red appeared for both errors and standard buttons. Drivers couldn't glance and act.

03
Broken Navigation & Mapping

The built-in map was unreliable and drivers didn't trust it — so they carried a second phone purely for navigation. The address search required manual entry of data the system already held.

"The navigation is bad! I don't trust it."
04
App Crashes & Instability

Frequent crashes during the most stressful moments — arriving at pickup, submitting amendments — destroyed driver trust and increased support call volume. Instability was both a technical and experience problem.

Information Hierarchy Diagram — current app IA with starred driver priorities
Competitor Analysis

What the best driver apps were already doing right

I benchmarked the leading ride-hailing driver apps across three key areas: how drivers explore available jobs, the pickup experience, and the earnings dashboard. Both platforms had solved glanceability far more effectively — large tap targets, clear hierarchy, status-first layouts.

The earnings pages in particular were revelatory: personalised, motivating, and data-rich. Drivers knew exactly where they stood against their weekly target at any moment.

03 — Strategy

Three principles to guide every decision

Rather than jumping to solutions, I defined design strategies that would act as a filter for every subsequent design choice — keeping the team aligned and the output coherent.

🗂

Restructure the information architecture

Make the most important information immediately accessible. Every feature should be reachable within 3 taps — no exceptions. The landing experience should reflect what drivers actually care about: jobs, earnings, and status.

👁

Design for glanceability & tappability

A driver cannot look at their phone for more than a second. UI elements must communicate at a glance. Tap targets must be thumb-friendly from one arm's reach. Standard patterns reduce cognitive load; consistency builds trust.

🤝

Make communication transparent

Help drivers achieve their goals — more jobs, better pay, stronger ratings — by giving them the data and tools they need. Reduce friction in every interaction between driver and support, driver and passenger, driver and system.

The old landing page was a company news feed. The new home screen greets the driver by name, shows the day's traffic and weather context, and surfaces live job demand data. One tap to go online.

The old heatmap was a satellite map with no context. The redesign introduced a scannable region list with Busy / Moderate / Quiet status tags, job counts, driver counts, and a redesigned airport queue screen with wait-time estimates.

Work Smarter, Not Harder

Eliminating unnecessary input by using data the system already has

Drivers were manually typing in parking locations, toll roads, and customer names — data the system already knew. The redesign pre-populated dropdowns, added tap-to-confirm parcel counts, and removed modal dialogs that interrupted the on-job flow.

05 — Outcomes

The results

Reduced support call volume

Clearer on-job flows, pre-populated forms, and inline confirmations eliminated many of the routine support calls that burdened the operations team daily.

Improved driver confidence

Drivers reported feeling more in control of their shift — knowing demand levels, understanding their earnings, and trusting the navigation enough to stop carrying a second device.

A foundation for ongoing iteration

The redesign established a component library and design system that the team could build on — reducing future design and development overhead significantly.

Reflection

What I learned

The environment is the brief

Designing for a driver on the road is fundamentally different from designing for someone at a desk. The constraint of one-handed use at arm's reach, under time pressure, changed almost every decision — from tap target size to information density to colour contrast. Getting in the car wasn't optional; it was essential.

Emotion is functional, not decorative

The tip notification and ratings features were initially deprioritised as "nice to haves." Research showed they were essential to driver motivation and retention. Emotional design isn't polish applied at the end — it's a core functional requirement that needs to be researched and designed with the same rigour as any other feature.

System thinking prevents local optimisation

Every design decision made for drivers had downstream effects on call center agents, support staff, and operations. The stakeholder map wasn't just a research deliverable — it was a constant reference point for evaluating trade-offs throughout the project.

Trust is earned incrementally

Drivers had been burned by the old app. Rebuilding trust required consistency — consistent patterns, consistent naming, consistent behaviour under load. A visually beautiful app that crashes is worse than an ugly one that works. Technical reliability and UX quality are not separable concerns.
