01 — The problem

When the business runs on spreadsheets
scale hits a ceiling

Onboarding worked — but only because Ops manually held everything together. The spreadsheet became the system of record, but only Ops could interpret it.

As the business expanded across countries, each onboarding introduced more compliance rules, more coordination, and more manual work.

The system wasn’t just slow — it was structurally unsustainable.

02 — Strategy

Not just a new tool for Ops — a faster onboarding system overall

Of course Ops needed a better internal tool. But the system was not only constrained by Ops capacity — it was also constrained by how easily employees and clients could move through onboarding without getting stuck.

So I approached the problem from both sides: improve internal throughput for Ops, while reducing friction across employee and client experiences so fewer cases stall or escalate in the first place.

Design onboarding as a self-serve system — not an Ops-supported workflow.
Core product stance

Employee

Clarify the next step, due dates, and required inputs so people can complete onboarding without guessing.

Client

Show accurate progress and shared task state so client teams can track onboarding without escalating.

Ops

Build a workspace that lets specialists review, decide, and move through operational queues quickly.

03 — Key design decision

Why I did not copy Offboarding Manager

Offboarding Manager

Finish one case end-to-end

Ops usually take one employee and work through the whole case. Tasks are connected and sequential — you cannot calculate severance before understanding leave taken, notice, and local rules.

  • One employee at a time
  • Dependencies between steps
  • Deep work on a single case
Onboarding Manager

Clear the same type of task across many cases

Onboarding has many employees moving through similar steps in parallel. Ops do not want to “finish John” first — they want to review all pending visa cases, then move to the next queue.

  • Many employees in flight at once
  • Parallel progress across tasks
  • Queue-based operational work

That insight shaped the rest of the product. Reusing Offboarding Manager would have been faster, but it would have optimised for the wrong way of working.

04 — Structure

A structure built around what needs to be done

How Ops move

Status becomes the entry point

Instead of starting from a person record and drilling down, Employee Manager starts from the work queue. Status is where the action happens: awaiting review, awaiting employee submit, compliance rejected, awaiting countersign.

Supporting layers

Task, lifecycle, and country add structure

Once Ops are in the right queue, task type, contract lifecycle, and country provide the context they need to work safely and fast. The structure supports specialist workflows without forcing them to jump between people.

05 — Designing the workflow

The system moves with Ops — not the other way around

One of the biggest inefficiencies in the original workflow was context switching. Approve a visa, then leave the current view, find the employee again, open the next step, and continue. The system kept making people relocate their work.

Employee Manager changed that. After making a decision, Ops can move directly into the next relevant action for the same employee — or stay in the current queue and continue reviewing. Either way, they remain inside the same operational context.

“Really? You can go directly to send the contract?”

That reaction from Ops told us the handoff flow was not just convenient — it made the tool feel like it actually understood their job.

06 — Shared visibility

Three surfaces, one underlying task reality

Employee view

See required tasks, due dates, and completion state in a way that reduces uncertainty and drop-off.

Client tracker

Track employee progress with the same underlying checklist so the client is not dependent on manual status updates.

Admin / Ops

See the same task state through an operational lens — prioritised, filterable, and ready for action.

This mattered because it aligned the whole system. Employees, clients, and Ops were no longer looking at different versions of onboarding. They were looking at the same task model, shaped differently for their needs.

08 — Lasting impact

The strongest proof was not launch day — it was what held afterwards

What I am most proud of is not only that Employee Manager shipped fast, but that the core decisions continued to work years later. The architecture remained in production as onboarding volume crossed 3,000 per month. Other teams looked to it as a reference. Ops training simplified from multi-platform walkthroughs to: log in to Employee Manager.

For me, that is the signal of a strong system: not just that it launches, but that it keeps working as the company grows.
